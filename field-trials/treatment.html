Keelson · ADME · video treatment

Sea Trials 01 · Seven Pages

A treatment for a video of about seven minutes. It shows Keelson, a rib for ADME, an OSDU MCP server and agents doing real work on a shared data platform. What runs today is filmed. What could come next is drawn.

A shared ADME instance, used by people from about a dozen companies, is run from a seven-page access guide. Three of its pages (who has access, who can reach which seismic subproject, which object id is which person) change with every invitation and grant. So the guide is rebuilt and emailed to everyone again each time. The work that could replace it is spread across repositories few people have seen: a Keelson rib, an OSDU MCP server, and the agent workflows building both.

This treatment proposes one story that takes the guide apart page by page. Each page is matched to what replaces it in the ADME tab today. The turn shows the factory that built those tools. The last chapter draws what could come next, ending with a guide that republishes itself. Alongside the full video there is a three-minute cut, plus short clips taken from it.

It does not present vision as product. Every scene carries a status stamp, and proposed work is drawn, never filmed. No real person, company, tenant or object id appears on screen. The rib runs on a sample instance cast from its design spec, and the MCP server runs on the open TNO and Volve datasets.

Audience and promise

The first audience is the people around ADME who know the platform and the pain but have not seen this work. That means colleagues who run or support instances, and the leadership they report to. Partner and public versions come later as clips on sample data, once the disclosure decisions below are made.

Logline. A shared ADME instance runs on a seven-page guide that is out of date the day it is sent. Keelson, a rib that knows the instance, an MCP server that knows OSDU, and agents that propose while people approve take the guide apart. Then the video turns around: the same harness is building the tools.
ViewerLeaves knowing
Colleagues who run or support ADMEThe operator's toil is specific, and most of what the guide says can be measured instead of maintained.

EngineersWhere each piece plugs in: the rib contract, the MCP server's five tool contracts (one registered today), and workflows as the unit of repeatable work.

Production

Form and devices

Acts I and IV are plates, drawn on the blueprint sheet with the series renderer. Acts II and III are real footage from the demo stage.

The signature device is the page-to-panel cut. A page of the sample guide lifts off the sheet and lands as the live panel that replaces it. The cut runs three times in Act II. It runs once in reverse in Next voyage, where the panels assemble into a guide that publishes itself.

Every frame carries a title block in its lower right corner with the strand name and a status: as built, in work, proposed, or plate. The words come from engineering drawing practice, which suits the blueprint identity.

Strand

The working title borrows the step after a hull is built. Sea trials take a ship into open water to prove it under real conditions. This would open a strand beside the numbered figures, with SEA TRIALS 01 on the title card and the same keel and ribs bookends.

Voice

Harper narrates, as in the series. A second voice reads the partner's side: an email that says every call returns 401, and a question to their own agent. That makes the partner a person on screen, not a row in a table.

Capture

The rig is the series rig. It uses an isolated Keelson home on port 7979 and a CDP screencast of the browser with the injected cursor. GitHub Copilot CLI is captured in the terminal, with the OSDU MCP server in its MCP config. Narration goes through tts.py, compose.py cuts the video, and qa.sh makes the contact sheet and a phone-width frame.

A · Narration draft

About 780 words, about five and a half minutes of speech, with the rest of the running time held for footage. Spoken forms are in appendix D.

TimeSceneNarration
0:00OpenerSea trials, one. Seven pages. This is the access guide for a shared ADME instance. Every time someone is invited or gets a grant, it's rebuilt, and sent to everyone again.
0:18Seven pagesThe instance is one platform where people from about a dozen companies test their systems against the same data. The guide does real work. How to connect. What each role can do. What a 401 means. Then three pages that are never true for long: who has access, who can reach which seismic subproject, and which object id is which person. Those pages come from Azure CLI calls, Graph queries and scripts, put together by hand. By the time the guide lands in an inbox, it's already behind.
0:58Confident and wrongSo why not point an AI model at the APIs and let it answer? Because the platform makes it easy to be confidently wrong. A malformed search comes back two hundred, with zero results and no error. Counts stop at ten thousand without saying so. And no model should invite anyone without a dry run. A smarter model isn't the fix. It needs better ground to stand on.
1:28The rigSo, three pieces. Keelson, a workbench for agents that runs locally. It keeps the record, runs workflows with approval gates, and serves its tools over MCP. A rib for ADME, bolted onto Keelson, that knows this instance: its people, its data, its seismic subprojects. And an MCP server that knows OSDU itself, so an agent gets bounded answers that say how sure they are. Then the guide comes apart.
2:02Who has accessPage four, who has access, is now the top of the ADME tab. From invitation to data: thirty-two people with entitlements, twenty-nine accepted, twenty-seven who can actually use it. The audit log adds who made a data call, company by company, in the guide's own words: invited, not used, idle, active. Measured a minute ago, not on the day the PDF went out. And when someone still wants the table, the rib writes it.
2:42Why 401Page three's troubleshooting table turns into a question about one person. Why does Rachel get a 401? The rib walks the checks in the order the platform applies them. Signed in. Right app. Right partition. Not in users at. There's the verdict, and a note to send her.
3:12Who can reach whatPage five, seismic access, becomes subprojects with their members by name. Pick a partner, and the rib lists exactly which subprojects they can read, with a note to copy. A grant starts as a dry-run plan: what changes, what's already true, what's blocked. Nothing is written until someone applies it.
3:47What's in itThe guide never said what data the instance holds. The tab does: records by legal tag, by the groups that can read them, and by kind, with the tags that need a look.
4:12Ask about itEvery panel has an explore button. It opens a chat that starts from what the panel measured. Ask for this week's partner update, and the agent writes it as a page.
4:40Ask the platformIn GitHub Copilot, the OSDU MCP server answers its first question: who am I on this platform, and is it healthy? Four more tools are on the way.
4:58The yardSo where did these tools come from? The MCP server is being built by Keelson. Each piece of work is a bead, with its dependencies. A workflow claims one, plans it, and stops at a gate until the plan is approved. Then it writes the code, runs the project's checks, opens a pull request, and three reviewers check correctness, conventions and coverage. Up to six run at once. Nineteen of the server's twenty-three merged pull requests came out of that workflow, and Keelson's own workflows wrote a hundred and four of Keelson's. The rib took another road: a clickable mockup first, then version one point oh to one point ten in three days.
5:50Next voyageHere's where it could go. Drawn, not built. The guide stops being a PDF. A workflow writes it from what the rib measured, shows what changed, waits for approval, and republishes it in place. Nobody gets it emailed again. A partner's own agent asks the MCP server, signed in as them: can I reach this subproject, and if not, why? The rib already has a seam for agent tools. Fill it, and an agent can stage a grant as a plan for the operator to apply. A weekly run joins facts the rib already measures but no single page holds, like a legal tag that expires before a cohort's passes end. And every question becomes an eval. A correct "I can't tell" passes. An invented answer fails.
6:40CloseAgents propose. People approve. And every answer says how sure it is. Keelson. Lay the keel, raise the ribs.
